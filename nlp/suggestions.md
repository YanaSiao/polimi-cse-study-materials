# Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Natural Language Processing is a super fun, highly practical course. Professor Carman and the TAs are incredibly chill and genuinely love classroom interaction. You won't have to wade through a mountain of deep math theory here; instead, the real meat of the course is all about **Transformers and LLMs**.



## Who should take this course?
* **Hands-on Learners:** You want a solid 50/50 split between practical application and a real group project.
* **Open-Source Fans:** You want to roll up your sleeves and work directly with state-of-the-art open-source LLMs.
* **The Non-Theorists:** You want to understand NLP without getting bogged down in theory as deeply as you would in *Advanced Deep Learning*.

## Who should NOT take this course?
* **Easy-A Seekers:** You’re just looking for a low-effort course to coast through for a high grade. 
* **Complete Novices:** You lack a basic foundation in Machine Learning theory.

## When to take it?
Taking this course during your **1st year (T2A track)** alongside the core *Machine Learning* course can be a fantastic challenge! It balances out perfectly: you make up for the lack of coding practice in the ML course, while using NLP to enrich your high-level ML understanding. Doing it this way sets you up beautifully, making *Artificial Neural Networks and Deep Learning (ANNDL)* a great ultimate card to add to your deck later. 

On the flip side, if you decide to take ANNDL first, keep in mind that NLP will feel like a total breeze afterward. Material-wise, it also naturally connects well with *Data Mining* and *Recommender Systems*.


## The Flaw: A Bit Too Wide, a Bit Too Shallow
In my personal opinion, the course has one major downside: **it tries to cover too much ground too quickly.** The curriculum attempts to cram in an overview of basic ML and ANNDL concepts in a very rushed manner, which isn't quite enough to build a solid foundation if you don't already know it. 

Because of this, I found myself a bit bored during the first three lectures, which killed the initial hype and momentum I had. I really wish they had developed the chapters on **LLMs, RAG, and text-to-audio** a bit more instead of spending time reviewing the basics.


## How to Manage the Group Project Well
The group project makes up **30%–40%** of your grade and is done in teams of 3 to 5 students. It kicks off in mid-April and wraps up at the end of May. 

* **The Workflow:** The project requires some intense teamwork right at the very beginning and at the absolute end. However, the middle 3 to 4 weeks are easily manageable in parallel because the system components are largely independent. You can split up the tasks and work synchronously!
* **Pro-Tip for Success:** Search for previous years' video presentations and GitHub repositories to see what works. To give you a head start, you can check out my team's system (Group AJJY):
  * **Our GitHub Repo:** [YanaSiao/RAG-Agent-System](https://github.com/YanaSiao/RAG-Agent-System)
  * **Our Video Presentation:** [PoliMillionaire Project video](https://youtu.be/dd0CIeuVRbs?si=Mugl_eLCpPW-M7X4)


## How to Prepare for the Theory Exam
The exam accounts for **60%–70%** of your final grade. The format is 65 multiple-choice questions plus 5 open-ended questions.

* **The Strategy:** The best way to ace this is to completely master the past exam questions from the `exam-questions.md` file in this folder. The professors recycle a lot of questions, only adding a few new ones every once in a while.
* **The Trap:** Watch out for options like *"all other options are correct"* or *"all other options are wrong"*. They are a classic double-edged sword—a total blessing if you know your stuff, but a curse if you're second-guessing yourself!


## Exercise Sessions and Labs
The labs are held by two PhD students who go through the Google Colab notebooks block by block. Your strategy here totally depends on your personal learning style:

* **If you prefer live coding and hacking things on your own:** Just code at your own pace! Skip the live sessions and only watch the recordings if you hit a wall or run into a weird bug.
* **If you prefer step-by-step guidance:** Definitely attend the live lab sessions. They will guide you slowly, line by line, ensuring you don't get lost.

Both approaches work great—just choose the one that matches how you learn best!


## Personal Opinion on the Course
Professor Carman is an awesome, easy-going, and fast-paced lecturer. I absolutely loved the abundance of real-world examples, jokes, and memes thrown into the slides. While I do wish there was a more serious and punishingly difficult version of this course available, I still enjoyed this one a whole lot!
